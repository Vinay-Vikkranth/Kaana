Methodology

Why This Works

Every question you answered was designed to reveal a specific dimension of your preference. Here's the research and reasoning behind the system.

Research by Chua et al. (2020) — studying 847 diners across Singapore — found that dining occasion is the master variable in restaurant choice. It doesn't just change one preference; it reorganises the importance ranking of every other attribute simultaneously.

A single scene tap captures five signals at once: price orientation, social context, format preference, adventurousness baseline, and local engagement — far more efficient than asking those five questions separately.

The Food Neophobia Scale (Pliner & Hobden, 1992) — the gold-standard psychological instrument for measuring willingness to try new foods — uses exactly 10 items. Psychometricians established that 10 items is sufficient to reliably capture a complete personality dimension.

Active learning research (Nature Scientific Reports, 2025) confirms that 8–12 carefully selected items capture 80%+ of the preference signal available from unlimited data. Beyond that, each additional question yields rapidly diminishing returns — and a worse user experience.

Each dish was selected to cover a distinct region of the 8-dimension taste space — no two dishes have the same signal pattern, so every card adds genuinely new information.

Binary systems (like/dislike) collapse two completely different signals: "I have a shellfish allergy" and "I just had seafood for lunch" both become a thumbs-down, but they mean completely different things.

The three-state system separates them precisely: Never (permanent hard boundary, weight −0.8) vs. Not Tonight (contextual skip, weight 0 — completely ignored in scoring) vs. Want (positive signal, weight +1.0).

The asymmetry (−0.8 not −1.0 for "never") is deliberate: negative food signals are slightly overcalibrated in self-report, so we soften them to compensate — a principle from loss aversion research.

Revealed Preference Theory
Samuelson (1938), extended in consumer behaviour research — reactions to concrete stimuli are more predictive than answers to abstract self-report questions.

Version 1 (Tasting Table) uses absolute ratings — you react to each dish independently. The three-state system captures nuance that binary systems miss, and the scene selection adds explicit contextual grounding upfront. Produces more balanced, nuanced profiles.

Version 2 (The Duel) uses forced pairwise comparison — eliminates response bias, faster, more game-like. Produces more decisive, polarised profiles.

Neither is objectively better — they use different psychometric instruments measuring the same thing. An ideal system would combine both.
