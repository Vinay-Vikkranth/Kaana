Methodology

Why This Works

The story felt casual. The science behind it is anything but. Here's what each chapter was actually measuring.

When people know they're being assessed, they perform. Ask someone "how adventurous are you as an eater?" and they say 7 — because nobody wants to say 3. This is called social desirability bias, and it poisons most food preference questionnaires.

A story bypasses this entirely. When you're making decisions for a character in a narrative — even one that's obviously you — you engage System 1 thinking (fast, instinctive, honest) rather than System 2 (slow, analytical, performative). Your choices reflect genuine preference, not a curated self-image.

This approach is grounded in scenario-based preference elicitation, widely used in behavioural economics to measure willingness-to-pay and consumer preference without the distortions of direct questioning.

Chapter 1 — Who's joining you?
Directly captures social_context and format_preference. A solo night implies focused, solo-friendly dining. Family implies inclusive, noise-tolerant settings. The companion choice also modulates price_orientation — date night correlates strongly with higher spend willingness.

Chapter 2 — How are you feeling?
Captures mood-driven adventurousness. "Exhausted" → familiar, reliable food. "Celebratory" → format_preference spikes to fine dining. This is the occasion modifier that research (Chua et al., 2020) shows reorganises every other preference.

Chapter 3 — What did you search?
The search query is a clean proxy for local_engagement and adventurousness. "Hidden gem locals love" is a 9/10 on local engagement. "Something I've never tried" is a 9/10 on adventurousness. People reveal their search behaviour honestly because it feels mundane.

Chapter 4 — The 45-minute wait.
A patience/quality signal. Willingness to wait at the bar correlates with price_orientation (high-end places have waits) and format_preference (bar culture = higher-end venues). "Use this to try somewhere new" fires adventurousness again — a cross-validation of chapter 3.

Chapter 5 — The specials board.
The highest-signal chapter. One concrete food choice simultaneously probes spice_tolerance, health_orientation, adventurousness, flavor_complexity, and local_engagement. The framing ("the server points to the board, you must choose") removes social escape — there's no "I'd have whatever" option.

Chapter 6 — The bill.
Direct price ceiling calibration. $95 for two is a specific, concrete amount that anchors the response. Vague questions like "how much do you usually spend?" produce inaccurate answers; a specific scenario produces reliable ones. This is the final price_orientation refinement.

After all 6 chapters, each taste dimension has a score (0–10) computed as the average of all signals that chapter choices contributed to it. The system then checks the top 2–3 dimensions and maps them to one of 7 archetypes:

The Explorer — adventurousness ≥ 7 + local_engagement ≥ 6. Seeks undiscovered local gems and unfamiliar cuisines.

The Connoisseur — price_orientation ≥ 7 + flavor_complexity ≥ 7. Quality over everything. Understands that a great meal is worth paying for.

The Social Feaster — social_context ≥ 7. Food is a vehicle for connection. Communal tables, shared plates, long evenings.

The Health Seeker — health_orientation ≥ 7. Eats with intention. Fresh, nourishing, purposeful.

The Comfort Lover — adventurousness ≤ 4 + health_orientation ≤ 4. Reliable and satisfying. The classics are classics for a reason.

The Value Hunter — price_orientation ≤ 3. Finds quality at any price point. Doesn't conflate cost with quality.

The Balanced Eater — no dominant dimension. Mood, occasion, and company all factor in equally. Impossible to predict, which makes them interesting.

Scenario-Based Preference Elicitation / Stated Preference Methods
Louviere, Hensher & Swait (2000) — Stated Choice Methods. Cambridge University Press. Establishes that scenario-based choice tasks produce more reliable preference data than direct rating or ranking.
System 1 / System 2 Thinking
Kahneman (2011) — Thinking, Fast and Slow. Story-mode decisions engage System 1, which accesses genuine preference without the self-editing of analytical System 2 thought.
Customer Restaurant Choice: An Empirical Analysis
Chua et al. (2020), PMC7503372 — food quality (25.2%), price (16.4%), service (15%), atmosphere (13.1%). Chapter 2 (mood) directly encodes the occasion modifier this paper identifies as the master variable.
Food Neophobia Scale
Pliner & Hobden (1992) — Chapter 3 (search query) and Chapter 5 (specials) are scenario-based proxies for the trait-level food neophobia this instrument measures.
Social Desirability Bias in Self-Report Surveys
Tourangeau & Yan (2007), Psychological Bulletin — Direct food preference questions are subject to strong presentation effects. Embedding identical questions in narrative scenarios suppresses this bias significantly.

V1 — Tasting Table: Absolute ratings. Three-state reactions capture nuance (want / not tonight / never). Best for users who want to deliberate and reflect. Produces balanced, nuanced profiles.

V2 — The Duel: Pairwise forced choice. Eliminates response bias via relative comparison. Fastest method. Produces decisive, polarised profiles with less nuance.

V3 — The Evening: Scenario-based narrative. Bypasses social desirability bias by embedding preference questions in realistic life situations. Slowest method but highest ecological validity — the choices most closely reflect what users would actually do in real life. Produces the richest contextual profiles.

The ideal system combines all three: V3 for initial profiling (highest accuracy), V1 for ongoing refinement (nuance), V2 for quick recalibration (speed). Each instrument captures something the others miss.
