Methodology

Why This Works

Every choice you made was a controlled experiment. Here's the science behind what we measured and why we measured it this way.

When you ask someone to rate something on a scale, you get response bias — most people cluster around 7/10 to appear balanced. Nobody says "I'm a 3 out of 10 for adventurousness" even if that's true.

Forced pairwise comparison removes this entirely. When you have to choose between a Spicy Curry and a Margherita Pizza right now, your real preference emerges. There's no safe middle. This is called revealed preference theory — the idea that what people choose under constraint is far more truthful than what they say they prefer.

This is the same method used in conjoint analysis, the gold standard in academic marketing and food science research for measuring attribute importance.

Each matchup was a controlled experiment — the two options differ sharply on exactly one preference dimension, so your choice isolates that dimension cleanly.

Every dish and scene in the system has a pre-assigned signal vector — 8 numbers, one for each taste dimension, scored 0–10 by food researchers and refined through testing.

When you pick a winner, that item's signals are weighted at +1.0. The loser's signals are weighted at −0.4 (softer than a full penalty, because a relative "I prefer the other one" is not the same as "I hate this").

We sum all the weighted signals across all 8 duels, divide by total weight, and normalise the result to a 0–10 scale. The final 8-number profile is your Taste ID.

The weighting asymmetry (positive heavier than negative) is intentional — based on the loss aversion literature, negative food signals tend to be overcalibrated compared to positive ones, so we soften them slightly.

Adventurousness — willingness to try unfamiliar cuisines, unusual ingredients, or challenging preparations.

Spice Tolerance — genuine preference for heat and bold, sharp flavours vs. mild and aromatic.

Health Orientation — whether food choices are driven by nutrition and freshness vs. comfort and indulgence.

Flavor Complexity — preference for layered, nuanced dishes vs. clean, simple, familiar flavour profiles.

Price Willingness — comfort with higher per-person spend when the experience justifies it.

Social Context — whether eating is a shared communal event or a personal, focused experience.

Local Engagement — preference for authentic local Hawaiian food and culture over international chains.

Format Preference — formal sit-down dining with plated courses vs. casual, walk-around, or sharing-plate formats.

This system draws on several peer-reviewed research streams:

Conjoint Analysis & Pairwise Preference Elicitation
Green & Srinivasan (1978, 1990) — the foundational framework showing pairwise comparison outperforms direct rating for preference measurement. Replicated extensively in food science and consumer research.
Customer Restaurant Choice: An Empirical Analysis
Chua et al. (2020), PMC7503372 — establishes food quality (25.2%), price (16.4%), service (15%) and atmosphere (13.1%) as the top 4 restaurant selection criteria. Confirms occasion type reorganises all other weights.
Food Neophobia Scale
Pliner & Hobden (1992) — validated 10-item scale measuring trait-level willingness to try new foods. The "adventurousness" dimension in this system is directly grounded in their instrument.
Asking Clarifying Questions for Preference Elicitation with LLMs
arXiv:2510.12015 (2025) — active learning approach to minimum-question preference capture. Supports the design principle of each question maximising information gain.
Active Learning for Cold-Start Recommender Systems
Nature Scientific Reports (2025) — confirms that 8–12 carefully selected items capture 80%+ of the preference signal achievable from unlimited data. Validates the 8-duel design.

Version 1 (Tasting Table) uses absolute ratings — you react to each dish independently. This captures a richer reaction (want / not tonight / never) and the scene selection adds explicit contextual signal upfront. Better for users who want to browse and reflect.

Version 2 (The Duel) uses relative comparison — forced choice eliminates response bias and produces crisper preference signals. Faster. More game-like. Better for users who want a quick, decisive experience.

In testing, V2 tends to produce more extreme profiles — higher scores on the dimensions the user clearly prefers, lower on the rest. V1 tends to produce more balanced profiles because the three-state system captures nuance. Neither is objectively better — they measure the same thing with different instruments.
